How Wandroz scores neighbourhoods

Wandroz is early. This page explains exactly what today's scores are based on, what they leave out, and what changes as coverage grows — so you can decide how much to trust them.

Right now: London is the only automated, data-driven city

London borough scores come from the UK Home Office's official street-level crime data, published monthly at data.police.uk, and are recomputed automatically every time the pipeline runs — no manual editing.

Turin and Zurich are also on the site as an early preview, with interactive maps using each city's real official neighbourhood boundaries (Città di Torino's "Quartieri" dataset; Stadt Zürich's "Statistische Quartiere" dataset) — but their safety ratings are a manual, illustrative first pass, not yet backed by an automated open-data feed the way London is. Treat Turin/Zurich as a preview of the map experience, not a verified rating, until they move onto a real pipeline.

How a score is built

For each London borough, Wandroz downloads every recorded crime for the most recent month available, within roughly a 1-mile radius of the borough's centre. Each category of crime is weighted by how relevant it typically is to a traveller's sense of safety — violent and sexual offences, robbery, and weapons possession count for more than, say, shoplifting or anti-social behaviour, which are heavily over-represented in the raw data relative to serious crime. The weighted total is then divided by the borough's resident population (2021 census), so a busier but more populous borough isn't unfairly penalised just for having more people in it.

Boroughs are then ranked against each other — relative to the other London boroughs currently covered, not against some fixed, borough-independent scale, and not against any other city yet.

What this method gets wrong today

Resident population, not visitor/daytime population. Central, nightlife-heavy or highly-visited boroughs can end up looking "less safe per resident" simply because relatively few people are officially resident there compared to how many people actually pass through on a given night. A proper fix (weighting by daytime or "workday" population instead of only resident population) is planned but not built yet — treat scores for very central boroughs with that in mind.
Crime data is still queried as a circle, even though the map now shows the real boundary. Each borough page now displays its actual administrative outline (ONS 2021 boundaries) instead of a plain circle, so the shape you see is accurate. The underlying crime count, however, is still fetched as a point plus roughly a 1-mile radius around the borough's centre, not filtered to the exact polygon — so incidents right at the edge of a borough may still be attributed to the wrong side. Fetching crimes strictly inside the real polygon is the next step.
One month at a time. Scores reflect the single most recent month the UK Police API has published, not a rolling average — so a single unusual month can swing a borough's relative position more than a longer-term trend would. Averaging several months is planned as more monthly data accumulates.
No day/night split yet. The official data doesn't include time-of-day, so today's score doesn't distinguish "safe to walk through at 3pm" from "safe to walk through at 3am" — it's one number for the whole day.

How this scales beyond London

Wandroz is meant to eventually cover neighbourhoods across Europe, not just London. Getting there honestly means being upfront about which of three tiers a city's data currently sits at:

TierWhat it means
1 — Official, automatedA government or police API/dataset exists and is pulled automatically on a schedule, with no manual editing. London is here today.
2 — Local press signalNo clean official feed exists yet, so coverage is built (semi-automatically) from local news and public safety reporting instead.
3 — Community-verifiedResidents confirm or correct scores directly, with multiple independent confirmations required before a correction is applied — to avoid the kind of single-person bias that has undermined similar crowdsourced safety apps in the past.

Every city page will eventually say plainly which tier its data comes from, rather than presenting every score as equally rigorous.

Updates

London's data refreshes automatically once a month via a scheduled job that re-pulls the latest official data, recomputes every score, and republishes the site — no manual editing involved.

Feedback

Wandroz doesn't have a public feedback form yet. If something here looks wrong, that's useful to know — this page will get a proper reporting mechanism as the site matures.
